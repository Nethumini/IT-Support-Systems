## 2.1 Chapter Overview

This chapter examines the literature that underlies the problem, the research gap and the design direction established in Chapter 1. Its purpose is not to catalogue what has been published on IT support automation, but to test whether the evidence actually supports the four claims on which this research rests: that the binding constraint in enterprise IT support is the cost of applying knowledge the organisation already holds; that existing automation stops short of the point at which that cost is incurred; that the technologies required to go further are individually mature; and that composing them into a governed, end-to-end system remains an open problem.

The review is organised into four analytical movements. Section 2.3 establishes the IT Service Management (ITSM) domain and positions this research within it, distinguishing the end-user support tier that this study addresses from the infrastructure operations tier that most published agentic work targets. Section 2.4 critically analyses five families of existing system — traditional tiered ticketing and static routing, supervised machine learning applied to the ticket record, LLM-based incident diagnosis, retrieval-augmented support recommenders, and integrated agentic IT support — assessing for each what it achieves, what constrains it, and whether it is suitable for the specific problem stated in Chapter 1. Section 2.5, the principal analytical section, examines the enabling technologies at three levels: the algorithms that would perform the work, the architectures that would organise it, and the workflows through which support is actually delivered. Section 2.6 synthesises these findings into a research gap that follows from the evidence rather than preceding it.

Two constraints govern the selection of literature throughout. First, only work that explains, compares, critiques or justifies an element already present in Interim Submission 01 and Chapter 1 is included; literature is not admitted because it is topical. Second, every assessment is required to answer the question *what does this finding mean for the proposed system* — where an approach is adopted, the adoption is justified from evidence, and where an approach is rejected, the specific limitation that disqualifies it is named. Citations follow the IEEE numeric scheme continued from Chapter 1, so that a given source carries the same number throughout the thesis.

## 2.3 Domain Overview

IT Service Management is the set of practices through which an organisation delivers, supports and improves IT services. It is organised around defined processes, principally incident management, request fulfilment, problem management and change management, and is instrumented by ticketing systems that record each request and by service level agreements that bound the time within which it must be handled. A systematic review of 47 articles drawn from leading journals and conferences finds that ITSM adoption delivers measurable benefits in service quality, process standardisation and customer satisfaction, while documenting a set of challenges that recur across implementations — among them the organisational cost of sustaining process discipline and the difficulty of establishing and maintaining the knowledge assets on which the processes depend [1]. The significance of that second finding is easy to understate. ITSM frameworks assume the existence of documented, current and findable resolution knowledge; the review records that producing and sustaining that knowledge is itself an unresolved problem of practice rather than an administrative detail [1].

Support is delivered through a tiered escalation structure, conventionally L1 → L2 → L3, in which generalists filter incoming work before it reaches specialists. The economics of this arrangement are sound, but it imposes two costs that recur throughout this review: queuing delay at each tier, and the loss of context at each handoff, since the information a user supplies at L1 is rarely sufficient for L2 and must be elicited again. That these costs are real and measurable is visible in the incident record itself: work on resolution-time prediction finds that process-intensity attributes such as the cumulative count of updates to an incident record (`sys_mod_count`) and the number of times an incident has been reassigned between support groups (`reassignment_count`) are among the strongest predictors of how long an incident will take to resolve [5]. Reassignment, in other words, is not a neutral routing event; it is a measurable determinant of duration.

The operating environment has grown steadily less favourable to this model. Enterprise estates are heterogeneous by construction — cloud services, on-premises infrastructure, varied endpoint hardware and layered software — and recent work on enterprise IT support characterises the consequence as long-tail failure modes that are difficult to resolve centrally, with faults frequently emerging from subtle local interactions rather than clear global failures [2]. Against this, a substantial share of the incoming workload is repetitive: password and access issues, connectivity faults, endpoint performance degradation and peripheral failures recur continuously, and the resolutions largely already exist in the organisation's records. The controlled evidence that this is the binding constraint comes from outside ITSM research proper: a staggered field deployment of a generative conversational assistant across 5,179 support agents produced a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour, comprising a 34% improvement for novice and low-skilled agents and minimal effect on the most experienced, an effect the authors attribute to the dissemination of the working practice of more able agents [3]. The distributional shape of that result is what makes it informative: if the limiting factor were generative fluency, experienced agents would have gained comparably; because the gain concentrates where organisational knowledge is least internalised, the constraint is access to knowledge rather than the ability to express it.

A neighbouring field must be distinguished at this point, because failing to do so is a common source of overstated claims. **AIOps** — artificial intelligence for IT operations — addresses failure perception, anomaly detection, root cause analysis and assisted remediation at the level of infrastructure and services, drawing on metrics, logs and traces produced by running systems. A survey of 183 papers published between January 2020 and December 2024 documents the rapid expansion of LLM-based methods across these tasks, together with persistent difficulties in evaluation consistency, data-source coverage and integration with existing toolchains [19]. The present research is *not* an AIOps study. It operates at the end-user support tier, where the trigger is a person describing a problem in natural language rather than a monitoring signal, where the evidence is conversational and often visual rather than telemetric, and where the remediation target is a single user endpoint rather than a service cluster. This distinction matters throughout the chapter: results obtained on infrastructure operations tasks bound what can be claimed at the support tier, but they do not transfer to it directly, and Section 2.4 treats them accordingly.

**Author's assessment.** The domain literature supports a specific and narrow reading of the problem, and it is worth stating that reading precisely because a looser one is easy to adopt. The difficulty in enterprise IT support is not an absence of technical knowledge, nor a deficiency of infrastructure, but the cost of retrieving and applying knowledge the organisation already possesses, incurred afresh on every recurrence and compounded by a tiered structure that repeatedly discards context [1], [3], [5]. This framing is what makes the proposed system's emphasis on retrieval grounding and end-to-end continuity a response to the evidence rather than a technology preference. It also sets the standard against which the remainder of this chapter judges existing work: an approach is relevant to this research to the extent that it reduces the cost of applying existing knowledge at the point where a user actually needs it.

## 2.4 Existing Systems / Frameworks / Designs

This section analyses five families of existing approach. They are treated in the order in which they extend the workflow: from systems that only record work, through systems that classify or predict it, to systems that diagnose it, ground it in organisational knowledge, and finally act on it.

### 2.4.1 Traditional tiered ticketing and static routing

Conventional ITSM platforms provide the system of record: a ticket is created, categorised, prioritised, assigned to a queue, escalated when a tier cannot resolve it, and closed. Automation in this family is predominantly rule-based — routing tables keyed on category, priority matrices, and SLA timers.

The strength of this arrangement is accountability. Every action is recorded, ownership is unambiguous, and SLA compliance can be audited, which is precisely why the model persists. Its limitation is that the platform records work rather than performing it. The incident data used in resolution-time research makes this visible: incidents accumulate updates, reassignments and reopenings, and those process-intensity attributes are among the strongest predictors of duration [5]. A routing rule that assigns on category alone cannot take account of specialisation, current workload, or the nuance of what the user actually reported, and the resulting misassignment is absorbed as reassignment churn. Enterprise platforms have begun to incorporate agentic capability, but characteristically as centrally orchestrated agents operating within predefined automation boundaries rather than as situated diagnosis and action at the endpoint [2].

**Suitability.** Not suitable as a solution, but indispensable as a substrate. The proposed system retains ticketing, lifecycle states, SLA tracking and audit logging precisely because the accountability properties of this family are genuine and must not be lost when intelligence is added on top of them.

**Author's assessment.** The common criticism that ticketing systems are "merely digital ledgers" is accurate but incomplete, and the incomplete version leads to a design error. A ledger is exactly what an auditable support process requires; the deficiency is not that the ledger exists but that nothing between ticket creation and human intervention reduces the work. This is why the proposed design places the Ticket Status Agent as a deterministic state machine rather than replacing lifecycle management with generative reasoning — the accountability function of the ledger is preserved, and intelligence is added at the points where work is actually performed.

### 2.4.2 Supervised machine learning applied to the ticket record

The dominant academic response to repetitive support work has been to apply supervised learning to ticket text and metadata. Oliveira, Nogueira and Brito compare six algorithms for incident-ticket classification using text mining and natural language processing, reporting 93.12% accuracy for a linear support vector classifier, ahead of stochastic gradient descent (90.01%), logistic regression (88.65%) and multinomial naïve Bayes (85.03%) [4]. Mulyati et al. extend the same paradigm from categorisation to timing, showing that a random forest regressor predicting incident resolution time attains R² = 0.8318 with a mean absolute error of 60.67 hours when features are aggregated across the incident lifecycle, against R² = 0.5412 when only the attributes available at ticket-open time are used [5].

Both results are methodologically careful, and both are stronger than they first appear because their authors report what constrains them. Oliveira et al. show that random oversampling materially improves performance and that the smaller datasets to which it was not applied perform appreciably worse [4] — accuracy is therefore a property of the corpus and its preparation, not of the algorithm. Mulyati et al. identify `sys_mod_count`, `assignment_group` and `reassignment_count` among the most predictive features [5], which is to say that the model's strength derives from process history accumulated *after* the ticket was raised.

**Suitability.** Partially suitable, and instructive in a way that is easy to miss. These techniques establish that ticket categorisation and duration estimation are tractable, which supports the Ticket Intelligence Agent and the predictive component of the proposed system. They do not address the research problem, because the model is a terminal artefact: it emits a label or a number and the process ends. It does not consult resolution knowledge, does not converse, and does not act.

**Author's assessment.** Two consequences follow for this research, and they pull in opposite directions. First, a reported accuracy of 93.12% [4] is not evidence that support automation is largely solved; it measures a sub-task that sits upstream of the work a support engineer performs, and the residual manual effort is untouched by it. Second, and less comfortably for the proposed design, the resolution-time finding implies that a predictor detached from the process that generates lifecycle features has structurally limited access to its strongest signal [5]. A system that integrates prediction with the conversation is therefore better placed than a standalone predictor — but only if its predictions are treated as advisory estimates that improve as the incident progresses, rather than as reliable forecasts issued at ticket creation. Chapter 1's predictive component is accordingly scoped as SLA-risk and resolution-time *estimation*, not as a proactive detection mechanism, and this is the evidence that requires that restraint.

### 2.4.3 LLM-based incident diagnosis systems

A more recent family applies large language models to diagnosis itself. Ahmed et al. conduct a large-scale study at Microsoft across more than 40,000 incidents from over 1,000 cloud services, evaluating GPT-3.x models in zero-shot, fine-tuned and multi-task configurations for recommending root causes and mitigation steps, and validating results with the actual incident owners [17]. Chen et al.'s RCACopilot goes further architecturally: it matches an incoming incident to a handler based on alert type, aggregates critical runtime diagnostic information, and only then invokes the model to predict a root-cause category and produce an explanatory narrative, achieving root cause analysis accuracy up to 0.766 over a year of real production incidents [18].

The strengths are substantial. Both systems operate on genuine production data rather than curated benchmarks, both report expert-validated results, and RCACopilot's diagnostic-information collection component has been in production use for over four years [18]. Two limitations are equally clear. The first is the performance level: a system correct in roughly three cases out of four is a competent assistant and an unacceptable autonomous actor, and neither system claims otherwise. The second is measurement: Ahmed et al. state explicitly that lexical metrics such as BLEU-4 are useful for comparing models against one another but that manual inspection and expert validation are required to assess actual performance [17].

**Suitability.** Directly relevant in architecture, not transferable in scope. The domain is cloud service incidents diagnosed by on-call engineers, not endpoint faults reported by end users, so the diagnostic evidence, the vocabulary and the resolution actions all differ. What transfers is the architectural principle demonstrated by [18]: assembling structured context *before* invoking the model materially improves the outcome relative to presenting a raw problem description.

**Author's assessment.** RCACopilot is the strongest single piece of architectural evidence in this review for the proposed design, and its contribution is often misread as a result about model quality when it is in fact a result about pipeline order. The same observation motivates retrieval grounding, and it is why the workflow in Chapter 1 performs image normalisation and knowledge retrieval *before* the troubleshooting dialogue rather than after it. Ahmed et al.'s methodological caution is equally consequential and is adopted directly in this research: automatic text-similarity metrics will not be treated as evidence of resolution quality, because the source that used them at the largest scale available states that they cannot bear that weight [17].

### 2.4.4 Retrieval-augmented support recommenders

A fourth family grounds generation in organisational knowledge. Toro Isaza et al. describe a deployed system built for an IT support client, combining RAG-based answer generation with an encoder-only classifier and a generative query-formulation stage, designed explicitly around two client constraints: incomplete domain coverage of the knowledge base, and restricted model size where the organisation declines larger proprietary models on cost and privacy grounds [11]. Xu et al., working on LinkedIn's customer service ticket corpus, show that conventional RAG treats past tickets as flat text and thereby discards intra-issue structure and inter-issue relations; constructing a knowledge graph from historical issues and retrieving sub-graphs improved retrieval by 77.6% in mean reciprocal rank and, over approximately six months of production deployment, reduced median per-issue resolution time by 28.6% [12].

These are the two most directly relevant systems in the review, and they establish something the general RAG literature cannot: that retrieval grounding survives contact with real enterprise support data under real constraints. The LinkedIn result is particularly valuable because it reports an *operational* outcome — resolution time — rather than only a retrieval metric [12]. The limitations are correspondingly specific. Both systems terminate at a recommendation; neither executes anything. Toro Isaza et al.'s reported evaluation is preliminary and centred on whether a retrieved link is correct [11]. And the knowledge-graph construction that produces the LinkedIn gains is a substantial engineering undertaking that presupposes a large corpus of historical tickets with recoverable structure [12].

**Suitability.** Suitable and adopted, with one component deliberately not adopted. Retrieval grounding over an organisational corpus is the mechanism by which the proposed system addresses the second specific problem of Chapter 1, and its value in this exact domain is evidenced by [11] and [12]. Knowledge-graph construction is excluded: it requires historical ticket volume and structural regularity that a synthetic, undergraduate-scale corpus cannot supply, and adopting it would risk reporting a benefit that derives from corpus properties rather than from the design.

**Author's assessment.** One lesson reported by Toro Isaza et al. deserves to be treated as a finding about this research rather than about theirs. Their first candidate dataset consisted of synthetic questions written by subject-matter experts from support document titles; comparison against genuine customer cases showed the real questions to be more verbose and to contain more off-topic noise, and the team consequently moved to actual support ticket data for training and validation [11]. Chapter 1 scopes this study to a synthetic dataset for reasons that remain valid — no ethically available route exists to production transcripts at this scale, and synthetic scenarios permit a defined ground truth. But [11] establishes that synthetic support queries are systematically *cleaner* than real ones, which means results obtained on them are optimistic with respect to deployment. This is a limitation of the present study that must be stated in the evaluation rather than discovered in it, and it is the literature, not caution, that requires the statement.

### 2.4.5 Integrated agentic IT support systems

The final family attempts the composed task. VIGIL deploys desktop-resident agents that perform situated diagnosis, retrieval over enterprise knowledge, and policy-governed remediation on user devices with explicit consent and end-to-end observability. Its edge layer comprises a planner agent that decomposes troubleshooting goals using ReAct-style deliberation, a diagnosis agent that gathers structured evidence through a bounded set of operating-system tools, a knowledge agent maintaining episodic and enterprise memory, and a remediation agent executing bounded, reversible interventions. All state-changing actions are mediated by a deterministic policy engine that classifies generated commands into allow, warn or deny tiers: low-risk actions execute automatically, moderate-risk actions require explicit user consent with a natural-language explanation of purpose and impact, and high-risk actions are blocked. A 10-week pilot on 100 endpoints reported a 39% reduction in interaction rounds, at least fourfold faster diagnosis, and self-service resolution in 82% of matched cases [2].

Alongside this sits benchmark evidence about how well agentic systems perform generally. ITBench evaluates agents across 102 real-world IT automation scenarios and finds that agents built on state-of-the-art models resolve only 11.4% of site reliability engineering scenarios, 25.2% of compliance and security operations scenarios and 25.8% of financial operations scenarios, with anomaly-detection tasks reaching an F1 of 0.35 [16]. Its failure analysis is as informative as its headline figures: by logging ReAct-style reasoning steps and tool invocations, the authors separate high-level reasoning errors from low-level tool errors, and report that smaller models produce markedly more invalid tool calls, syntax errors and repeated invocations, with agents over-relying on a single command-generation tool [16].

**Suitability.** VIGIL is the closest published analogue to the proposed system and confirms its central design premise: consent-gated, knowledge-grounded remediation on user devices is viable and valuable. It does not, however, close the gap identified in Chapter 1, and the reasons are stated by its own authors. The case-matching analysis covers only 39% of sessions, and unmatched cases may differ systematically in complexity; response quality was assessed by automated LLM-based evaluation rather than human expert judgement; the survey response rate was 23 of 100 participants, introducing self-selection bias; and the pilot ran on a single device category, so generalisation to heterogeneous fleets remains unvalidated [2].

**Author's assessment.** Three conclusions follow, and they are the load-bearing ones for this chapter. First, the existence of VIGIL means the research gap cannot be stated as the absence of an integrated system, and Chapter 1 accordingly states it as the absence of an *openly documented and independently evaluable* one — a smaller claim, but one that a reviewer cannot refute with a single citation. Second, VIGIL reports what its architecture achieved in aggregate but not which components produced the effect; its own observation that users rated the system *higher* when no historical knowledge-base match was available [2] shows that the relationship between retrieval and perceived value is not straightforward and cannot be inferred from a system-level outcome. Third, and most consequentially for design, VIGIL permits low-risk actions to execute automatically [2] while no adversarial evaluation of its policy layer is reported. Given the injection evidence analysed in Section 2.5.2, the proposed system takes the more conservative position of gating *every* remediation action on explicit user approval. This costs automation, and the cost is accepted deliberately: at undergraduate scale there is no fleet-level observability infrastructure to detect an incorrect automatic action after the fact, so the control that compensates for that absence must sit before execution rather than after it.

The first observation is that the five families do not compete — they occupy successive segments of one workflow and stop at different points. Ticketing records the request; supervised learning labels it; LLM diagnosis explains it; retrieval augmentation grounds the explanation in what the organisation already knows; and agentic systems act on it. Each family is competent within its segment, and each is evaluated on metrics internal to that segment: accuracy, R², mean reciprocal rank, root-cause accuracy. What no family except [2] and [12] reports is an outcome measured at the end of the workflow, which is where the user's problem is actually resolved. This is the single most important structural finding of the review, and it maps directly onto the six specific problems in Chapter 1: the shortcomings are not distributed randomly across the field, they are concentrated at the joints between these segments.

The second observation concerns the relationship between measured accuracy and admissible autonomy. Ranking the families by reported performance produces a misleading picture: supervised classification reports 93.12% [4], root-cause analysis 0.766 [18], agent benchmarks 11.4% [16]. These numbers are not comparable, because the tasks differ enormously in scope, and the apparent decline reflects task difficulty rather than technological regression. The comparison is nonetheless informative in one respect: performance falls as the task moves from labelling a record towards completing an operational outcome, which is consistent with the hypothesis that composition — not component capability — is the binding difficulty. This is the evidence for Chapter 1's claim that the deficiency is architectural.

The third observation is that the two families that report operational outcomes both did so in industrial settings with resources unavailable to this study — a six-month production deployment at LinkedIn [12] and a ten-week enterprise pilot at Amazon [2] — and both report those outcomes at system level. Neither publishes an architecture at a level of detail permitting reimplementation, and neither decomposes its result. For a researcher entering this area, the practical consequence is that the two most convincing pieces of evidence in the field are also the two least reusable. That asymmetry — strong outcomes, unavailable designs — is what an openly documented architecture addresses, and it is why the contribution claimed in Chapter 1 is openness and inspectability rather than superiority.

## 2.5 Technological Analysis

Section 2.4 established where existing systems stop. This section examines the technologies that would allow a system to continue, and evaluates them against the requirements that Chapter 1 derived from the problem. The analysis proceeds at three levels: the algorithms that perform the individual functions, the architecture that organises them, and the workflow through which the composed system delivers support.

### 2.5.1 Algorithmic Analysis

The proposed system requires algorithms for five distinct functions: understanding and classifying user language, retrieving relevant organisational knowledge, deciding what to do next, estimating resolution time and SLA risk, and interpreting images. Each is examined below against the alternatives available for the same function.

**Conversational understanding: supervised classification versus generative reasoning.** The function required at the front of the pipeline is to determine what a user is reporting and to sustain a diagnostic dialogue about it. Two algorithmic families are candidates. Supervised text classification over the ticket corpus is mature and measurable: a linear support vector classifier reaches 93.12% accuracy on incident tickets, with stochastic gradient descent, logistic regression and multinomial naïve Bayes trailing at 90.01%, 88.65% and 85.03% respectively [4]. Large language models offer a different capability profile: multi-step reasoning and sustained instruction-following that emerge above certain parameter scales and are shaped further by adaptation tuning [6].

The comparison turns on three properties rather than on accuracy alone. **Label space:** a supervised classifier is confined to the taxonomy it was trained on, whereas an LLM can categorise an issue expressed in vocabulary absent from any training corpus — a decisive difference for long-tail faults [2]. **Dialogue state:** a classifier assigns a label to a completed text, whereas the capability actually required is conditional adaptation, selecting the next diagnostic step from the reported outcome of the previous one [6], [13]. **Verifiability:** here the ordering reverses. A classifier's performance is measurable against held-out data and is stable across runs, whereas an LLM is documented as prone to fluent generation of confident but factually incorrect content, together with knowledge staleness and untraceable reasoning [6].

**Author's assessment.** Neither family is adequate alone, and the evidence indicates *where* each should be used rather than which should win. Generative reasoning is warranted where unconstrained user language must be interpreted, because that is the situation a fixed taxonomy cannot cover. Deterministic logic is warranted wherever an output feeds an accountable record, because that is where the LLM's documented unverifiability becomes a liability rather than an inconvenience. This is precisely the split Chapter 1 specifies — a Ticket Intelligence Agent combining rule-based and LLM logic, and a Ticket Status Agent implemented as a deterministic state machine — and Oliveira et al.'s corpus-dependence finding [4] adds a further reason for it: a purely supervised approach would require a labelled corpus of a size this study cannot ethically obtain, whereas an LLM classifier requires none.

**Retrieval: lexical, dense and structured.** Grounding requires retrieving the passages of organisational knowledge relevant to what the user described. Sparse lexical retrieval such as TF-IDF or BM25 matches terms directly and remains a strong baseline. Dense passage retrieval instead encodes questions and passages into a shared vector space using a dual encoder trained from a modest number of question–passage pairs, and outperforms BM25 by 9–19% absolute in top-20 passage retrieval accuracy [9]. The efficiency property that makes this practical at scale comes from the bi-encoder arrangement analysed by Reimers and Gurevych: because passages are encoded independently and compared by cosine similarity, finding the most similar pair among 10,000 sentences falls from approximately 65 hours using a cross-encoder formulation to roughly 5 seconds [10].

The relevance of the lexical–dense distinction to IT support is concrete rather than theoretical. A user writing "my internet keeps dropping" shares no meaningful terms with an article titled "Wireless adapter power management configuration"; lexical retrieval cannot connect them, and semantic retrieval can. This is the algorithmic justification for the vector-store component of the proposed system, and it is why the design specifies embeddings and cosine similarity rather than keyword search over the knowledge base.

A third option exists and is deliberately not taken. Xu et al. demonstrate that treating past tickets as flat text discards intra-issue structure and inter-issue relations, and that constructing a knowledge graph and retrieving sub-graphs improves mean reciprocal rank by 77.6% and reduced median per-issue resolution time by 28.6% in production [12]. This is the strongest retrieval result in the reviewed literature. **Author's assessment:** it is nonetheless the wrong choice for this study, and for a reason that is about corpus rather than about method. The gains in [12] derive from relations extracted across a large body of historical tickets authored by real support staff; a synthetic corpus constructed for this study would have whatever relational structure its author put into it, so any measured benefit would reflect the construction of the dataset rather than the merit of the technique. Adopting a method whose benefit cannot be honestly attributed would weaken the evaluation, not strengthen it. Structured retrieval is therefore recorded as a justified direction for future work rather than as a component of this system.

**RAG as a composition strategy.** The original formulation of retrieval-augmented generation pairs a parametric generative model with an explicit non-parametric memory that can be inspected and updated without retraining [7]. Gao et al. organise the subsequent development into three paradigms — naive RAG, which retrieves and concatenates; advanced RAG, which adds pre- and post-retrieval optimisation such as query rewriting and re-ranking; and modular RAG, which reconfigures the pipeline into interchangeable components — motivating the progression explicitly by the shortcomings of the naive form [8]. Domain instantiations show what this looks like under enterprise constraints: Toro Isaza et al. combine retrieval with an encoder-only classifier and a generative query-formulation stage specifically to compensate for incomplete knowledge-base coverage and for the smaller models that cost and privacy considerations impose on clients [11].

**Author's assessment.** The proposed system sits at the boundary between the naive and advanced paradigms, and this should be stated plainly rather than described aspirationally. Retrieval is performed once per technical message, before the troubleshooting dialogue, with keyword extraction from images feeding the same query path; there is no re-ranking stage and no iterative retrieval. This is defensible at the scale of the study because the knowledge corpus is curated and small, which is the condition under which naive retrieval performs acceptably [8]. It also means that any retrieval shortfall observed in evaluation is attributable to a known and stated design position rather than to an unexplained failure — which is a stronger position than claiming a more advanced pipeline than the implementation delivers.

**Deciding what to do next: reasoning–action interleaving versus bounded action selection.** The algorithmic question at the point of action is how the system converts a diagnosis into an operation. ReAct establishes the general answer: interleaving reasoning traces with actions allows a model to induce, track and revise plans while gathering information from external sources, outperforming imitation and reinforcement learning baselines by 34% and 10% absolute success rate on ALFWorld and WebShop respectively while using only one or two in-context examples [13]. The observation-feedback loop is what makes the approach robust — the model sees the result of each action and adjusts.

The counter-evidence concerns what happens when this loop is given an unbounded action space in an operational setting. ITBench logs ReAct-style reasoning steps and tool invocations separately, permitting high-level reasoning errors to be distinguished from low-level tool errors, and reports that smaller models generate markedly more invalid tool calls, syntax errors and repeated invocations, with agents over-relying on a single natural-language-to-command tool rather than balancing across the available toolset [16].

**Author's assessment.** These two findings are frequently read as being in tension; they are not. ReAct's contribution is the *control structure* — reason, act, observe, revise — and that structure is sound. What ITBench measures is the cost of pairing that structure with free-form command synthesis, where an incorrect token produces a malformed or wrong command that the environment nonetheless attempts to execute [16]. The design implication for this research is specific: adopt the reason–act–observe pattern, but replace natural-language command generation with selection from an enumerated whitelist plus validated parameters. Under that arrangement the model's error mode degrades from *executing the wrong command* to *proposing an inapplicable action*, which a validation layer rejects and a user declines. That is a categorical improvement in failure behaviour, and it is the algorithmic justification for the Action Executor Agent's design in Chapter 1.

**Predictive estimation.** The SLA and resolution-time component requires a supervised regressor over ticket and lifecycle features. The relevant evidence is unambiguous about both feasibility and its condition: a random forest regressor attains R² = 0.8318 with a mean absolute error of 60.67 hours on lifecycle-aggregated features, against R² = 0.5412 on features available at ticket-open time, with `sys_mod_count`, `assignment_group` and `reassignment_count` among the strongest predictors [5]. **Author's assessment:** the honest reading is that resolution-time prediction is well supported *as a progressively refined estimate* and weakly supported *as a forecast issued at ticket creation*, which is exactly when a support system would most like to have one. The proposed system should therefore present predictions as advisory signals that improve as the incident accumulates history, and the evaluation should report accuracy at more than one point in the lifecycle. Claiming proactive prediction at creation time would exceed what the evidence supports.

**Multimodal interpretation.** Chapter 1 specifies an Image Analysis Agent because users frequently capture faults accurately in screenshots that they describe poorly in prose. The supporting evidence is a multimodal LLM-based fault detection and diagnosis framework built on GPT-4-Preview, combining online and offline processing and using LLM-generated synthetic data to address imbalanced fault scenarios [24]. **Author's assessment:** the transfer must be claimed carefully. That framework addresses industrial equipment diagnosis, where the inputs are sensor streams and process imagery, not Windows error dialogues; what it establishes is that multimodal models can contribute usefully to fault interpretation, not that they perform well on end-user screenshots specifically. The defensible position — and the one Chapter 1 adopts — is architectural rather than performance-based: the image is converted to a textual description and keywords, which then follow the same classification and retrieval path as typed text. This confines the multimodal claim to a normalisation step whose contribution can be measured directly, and avoids asserting a diagnostic accuracy that the reviewed literature does not evidence for this input type.

**Measuring the result.** A final algorithmic question concerns how the system's quality will be judged, since the choice of metric determines what the research can claim. Ahmed et al. are explicit that lexical metrics such as BLEU-4 are useful for comparing model configurations against one another, but that manual inspection and validation with experts is required to assess actual performance [17]. RAGAs offers a complementary and more suitable instrument for the retrieval component: a reference-free metric suite decomposing RAG quality into faithfulness (whether the answer is supported by the retrieved context), answer relevance (whether it addresses the question asked) and context relevance (whether the retrieved passages are focused on the question), validated against human annotator judgements on a purpose-built dataset [25]. **Author's assessment:** the value of [25] for this research is that it separates three failure modes that a single end-to-end score conflates — retrieving the wrong passage, ignoring the right one, and answering a different question. Chapter 1 commits to reporting retrieval relevance separately from end-to-end resolution outcomes, and [25] provides the decomposition that makes that commitment operational, while [17] supplies the reason not to substitute either for expert judgement of whether a user's problem was actually solved.

**Algorithmic safety.** One algorithmic result belongs here rather than in the design section because it constrains what any algorithm can be asked to guarantee. AgentDojo reports that when a secondary attack-detector defence is deployed, prompt-injection attack success against tool-using agents falls to 8% — reduced substantially, but not to zero — and that current models solve fewer than 66% of its tasks even with no attack present [22]. ToolEmu, evaluating across 36 toolkits and 144 test cases, finds that even the safest agent evaluated exhibits failures in 23.9% of cases, with 68.8% of the failures surfaced by the emulator confirmed by human evaluation as genuine risks [23]. **Author's assessment:** these figures establish that detection-based safety is a mitigation, not a control. A residual 8% attack success rate is acceptable for an agent that drafts an email and unacceptable for an agent that executes a command on a working machine. The algorithmic conclusion is therefore that safety cannot be delegated to any model-side algorithm at all, which is the premise the next section develops architecturally.

### 2.5.2 Design Analysis

**Monolithic versus decomposed architectures.** The first architectural question is whether one model should perform every function or whether the work should be divided across specialised components. The agent literature provides a well-developed formulation of the second option: Wang et al. organise LLM-based autonomous agents around four modules — profile, memory, planning and action — and survey their construction, application and evaluation [14], while Liu et al. review 124 studies applying LLM-based agents across software engineering activities, covering multi-agent collaboration modes and human–agent collaboration alongside the reliability and evaluation challenges that remain open [15].

Decomposition offers three architectural benefits relevant to this research. It permits **specialisation**, so that each component's prompt, context and validation are fitted to one function. It permits **independent testing**, because a component with a defined input and output can be exercised in isolation — a property a monolithic prompt does not have. Most importantly for this study, it creates **explicit boundaries at which policy can be enforced**, since a boundary that exists in the architecture can carry a check, and one that exists only inside a prompt cannot.

The counter-argument is equally real and should be stated: decomposition adds orchestration complexity, multiplies the number of model invocations and therefore cost and latency, and creates failure modes at the interfaces between agents. The AIOps survey identifies exactly this concern, noting that the computational cost and time-efficiency of LLM-based solutions is a domain-specific challenge, and that a well-designed smaller model augmented by human expertise may achieve comparable results at a fraction of the cost [19].

**Author's assessment.** For a system whose remit ended at recommendation, that cost argument would likely be decisive, and a monolithic prompted assistant would be the rational design. It is not decisive here, because this system executes commands on user machines. Once execution is in scope, the governance property of decomposition stops being an architectural nicety and becomes the mechanism by which the system is made safe — the conversational component and the executing component must be separable in order for the second to be constrained independently of the first. The five-agent structure specified in Chapter 1 is justified on this basis, and the honest accounting is that it is paid for in latency and API cost, which the evaluation should measure rather than ignore.

**Where the human belongs in the design.** A criticism raised in the software engineering agent survey has unusual force for this research. Liu et al. observe that current LLM-based agent systems frequently *replace* human roles with agents rather than supporting effective human–agent collaboration, and that this reduces stakeholder involvement and trust [15]. VIGIL's own pilot supplies the complementary observation from the user side: participants identified the transparency of the system's actions and reasoning as critical to trust, while several requested elevated permissions for autonomous task completion [2].

**Author's assessment.** Read together, these findings reposition human-in-the-loop control from a safety concession to a design property with independent value. Chapter 1 justifies the approval gate on security grounds; [15] and [2] indicate that it also serves trust and user acceptance, and that transparency about what the system intends to do is part of what makes it acceptable. This has a concrete design consequence in the proposed system: the approval request must state which action will run, and why, in language the user can evaluate — an approval dialogue that presents an opaque command name would satisfy the security requirement while failing the trust requirement that [2] identifies. It is also worth registering the tension in VIGIL's own finding: users who trust the system begin asking for more autonomy [2]. A design that grants it in response would erode exactly the control that earned the trust, which is an argument for fixing the approval policy by design rather than by user preference.

**Centralised orchestration versus edge-resident agents.** VIGIL places its agents on the endpoint itself: a planner agent decomposing troubleshooting goals with ReAct-style deliberation, a diagnosis agent collecting structured evidence through bounded operating-system tools exposed via a tool protocol, a knowledge agent maintaining episodic and enterprise memory, and a remediation agent executing bounded, reversible interventions, with cloud agents providing fleet-level coordination, observability and governance [2]. The reported benefits of colocating reasoning with the environment are lower latency, access to fine-grained local context, and resilience under partial connectivity [2].

The proposed system takes a different position: orchestration is centralised in the backend, while execution occurs locally through the PowerShell engine. **Author's assessment:** the trade-off is real and runs partly against this research. An edge design has better access to device context, and VIGIL's results indicate that this access is where a meaningful part of its value originates — its authors note that users rated the system higher when no historical knowledge-base match existed, which they interpret as on-device reasoning adding value beyond knowledge lookup [2]. A centralised design forfeits some of that. What it gains is a single place where audit records, RBAC decisions and policy enforcement occur, which for a study whose contribution is an inspectable and independently evaluable architecture is the more important property: a reader can examine one enforcement path rather than reason about the behaviour of distributed agents. The choice is therefore justified by the research objective rather than by technical superiority, and that distinction should be stated rather than blurred.

**Enforcement outside the model.** The central design question for any system that acts is where the safety boundary sits. The evidence that it cannot sit inside the model is now substantial and comes from four independent directions. Greshake et al. show that adversarial instructions need not originate with the user, since content *retrieved* by an LLM-integrated application can carry the attack — an indirect vector that applies to any system performing retrieval over a corpus it does not fully control, demonstrated against real deployed applications [20]. Liu et al. establish the breadth of exposure empirically: of 36 real-world LLM-integrated applications tested with a black-box injection technique, 31 were susceptible, with 10 vendors subsequently validating the findings [21]. AgentDojo identifies the structural cause — language models operate directly on text and lack a formal mechanism for distinguishing instructions from data — and quantifies the limits of defence, with detector-based mitigation reducing attack success to 8% rather than eliminating it [22]. ToolEmu shows that the residual risk is not hypothetical, with even the safest evaluated agent failing in 23.9% of test cases [23].

VIGIL's design responds to the same evidence with a deterministic policy engine that evaluates generated commands against declarative policies and classifies them into allow, warn or deny tiers: low-risk actions execute automatically, moderate-risk actions require explicit user consent accompanied by a natural-language explanation of purpose and impact, and high-risk actions are blocked [2]. The proposed system adopts the same principle — enforcement in application code, outside the model — with a more conservative policy: an enumerated whitelist of non-destructive actions, parameter validation, risk tiering for presentation, and mandatory user approval for *every* remediation action.

**Author's assessment.** The difference between the two policies is a difference in what compensates for error. VIGIL can permit low-risk auto-execution because it operates a fleet-level observability layer capable of detecting and investigating an incorrect action after it occurs [2]. This study has no such layer, and constructing one is outside its scope. Where after-the-fact detection is unavailable, the control must precede execution — hence universal approval. The cost is a measurable reduction in automation, and it should be reported as such rather than presented as an unqualified virtue. What the arrangement does guarantee, and what no prompt-level defence can, is that nothing the model emits can extend the set of operations the application is capable of performing: an injected instruction can at most cause an in-whitelist action to be proposed, which the user must still approve.

**Design decisions and their justification.** The following table records the principal architectural decisions taken in Chapter 1, the evidence on which each rests, and the alternative that was considered and rejected.

| Design decision | Evidence base | Alternative rejected | Justification for the decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Five specialised agents rather than one prompted model | [14], [15]; governance argument from [20]–[23] | Monolithic LLM assistant | Separability is the precondition for constraining execution independently of conversation; cost and latency penalties are accepted and will be measured [19] |
| Deterministic state machine for ticket lifecycle | [4] on verifiability; [5] on lifecycle features; auditability requirement from §2.4.1 | LLM-driven status inference | Lifecycle state feeds SLA accounting and audit records; a decision that must be reconstructable requires re-executable rules, not a generative justification |
| Enumerated action whitelist with parameter validation | [16] on tool-call failure modes; [20], [21], [22], [23] on injection | Free-form command synthesis (natural language to shell) | Degrades the worst-case failure from executing a wrong command to proposing an inapplicable one; no model output can extend the executable set |
| Mandatory user approval for every remediation action | [2] on consent-gated remediation and trust; [22] residual 8% attack success; [23] 23.9% failure rate | Risk-tiered auto-execution of low-risk actions, as in [2] | No fleet-level observability exists at this scale to detect an incorrect automatic action after the fact, so the control must precede execution |
| Centralised orchestration with local execution | [2] on edge benefits; openness objective from Chapter 1 | Fully edge-resident agents | A single enforcement and audit path is inspectable and independently evaluable, which is the stated contribution; local context advantages are forfeited knowingly |
| Retrieval before dialogue, single retrieval pass | [7], [8] paradigms; [18] on context assembly before invocation | Post-hoc verification of generated advice; iterative retrieval | Grounding the response is more effective than correcting it afterwards [18]; a curated small corpus is the condition under which single-pass retrieval is adequate [8] |

### 2.5.3 Workflow Analysis

The final level of analysis concerns the process itself: how a problem travels from a user's report to a verified resolution, and where existing workflows break.

**The conventional workflow and its discontinuities.** In current practice the sequence is: the user reports an issue, possibly to a chatbot; a ticket is created; a human reads the ticket, gathers further context, consults documentation, decides on a course of action, performs it or instructs the user to perform it, and closes the record. Each transition is a discontinuity at which context is re-elicited and time is lost, and the incident record itself carries the evidence: update counts and reassignment counts are among the strongest predictors of how long an incident takes to resolve [5]. That the round-trip structure is itself a significant cost — rather than an unavoidable feature of support work — is demonstrated by VIGIL's pilot, which reduced interaction rounds by 39% and achieved at least fourfold faster diagnosis by situating diagnosis and remediation at the point where the fault occurs [2].

**How the reviewed families handle each workflow stage.** The comparison below assesses the five families against the stages that a complete support workflow must cover.

| Workflow stage | Tiered ticketing [1], [5] | Supervised ML [4], [5] | LLM diagnosis [17], [18] | RAG recommenders [11], [12] | Agentic systems [2], [16] | Proposed workflow |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Trigger / detection** | User-reported; manual intake | Ticket record, post-creation | Alert or incident record [18] | User query or case text | User-reported issue [2] | User message, text or screenshot |
| **Context assembly** | Manual, repeated at each tier | Feature extraction only | Automated aggregation before model invocation [18] | Retrieval over knowledge corpus | Local diagnostic tools plus retrieval [2] | Image normalisation, then retrieval before dialogue |
| **Analysis / diagnosis** | Human | None — labels or durations only | LLM-generated root cause and mitigation [17], [18] | Retrieved precedent presented to a human | Planner and diagnosis agents [2] | Grounded multi-turn dialogue |
| **Resolution / action** | Human executes | None | Recommendation only | Recommendation only | Policy-governed execution, tiered [2] | Whitelisted action, user-approved, then executed |
| **Verification** | Human judgement | Not applicable | Not addressed | Not addressed | Stepwise verification with rollback preference [2] | Result reported and logged after execution |
| **Escalation** | Tier-to-tier handoff, context lost | Not applicable | Human on-call retains ownership | Human agent retains ownership | Structured escalation to human operators [2] | Escalation detection in conversation; hand-off with ticket context retained |
| **Closure** | Manual | Not applicable | Not addressed | Not addressed | Not separately reported | Deterministic lifecycle transition, auto-closure and re-open detection |

Reading across the rows makes the field's structural deficiency legible in a way that reading any single paper does not. Four of the five existing families have empty cells at *resolution*, *verification* and *closure* — the stages at which the user's problem actually stops being a problem. Only the agentic family populates them, and it is also the family with the weakest independent evidence of general competence, resolving 11.4% of realistic SRE scenarios at benchmark level [16].

**Where context assembly belongs.** One workflow finding recurs and is adopted directly. RCACopilot aggregates critical runtime diagnostic information *before* invoking the model rather than presenting the model with a raw problem description [18], and VIGIL similarly assembles retrieved artefacts into a grounded context package from which a remediation plan is then synthesised [2]. Both place assembly before generation. The proposed workflow follows the same ordering — an attached image is converted to text and merged into the message, and knowledge retrieval occurs, before the troubleshooting dialogue begins. **Author's assessment:** this ordering is what converts retrieval from a fact-checking afterthought into a constraint on what is generated in the first place, and the consistency with which two independently developed production systems arrived at it is stronger evidence than either alone.

**A limitation of the proposed workflow that the literature exposes.** The AIOps survey observes that a large body of LLM-based root cause analysis work uses incident reports as the primary data source, and argues that this disrupts the automation flow because root cause analysis should be triggered by failure perception rather than by a written report [19]. Applied literally to this research, the criticism would be severe, since the proposed workflow is triggered by a user's message. **Author's assessment:** the criticism does not transfer intact, because it concerns infrastructure operations, where telemetry exists and can legitimately serve as a trigger. At the end-user support tier the human report *is* the detection event; there is no equivalent continuous signal from a user's frustration with a slow laptop. What the criticism does establish, correctly, is a bound on the claim this research may make: the system is reactive by construction at the conversational tier, and its predictive components estimate SLA risk and resolution time for incidents already raised rather than detecting problems before they are reported. Chapter 1 scopes the predictive element in exactly those terms, and [19] is the reason that scoping is correct rather than modest.

**Verification and closure.** VIGIL executes remediation incrementally, verifying after each step, adapting its plan or escalating if health signals regress, and preferring reversible actions to enable rollback [2]. The proposed workflow is weaker here: it executes an approved action, reports the result, writes an audit record, and passes control to the Ticket Status Agent for closure or escalation. **Author's assessment:** this is a genuine gap between the proposed design and the state of the art, and it should be acknowledged rather than obscured. Two considerations bound its severity. First, the whitelist is restricted to non-destructive diagnostic and cleanup operations, so the class of failures requiring rollback is deliberately small. Second, closure is not automatic on execution: lifecycle transitions remain under the Ticket Status Agent, which is where re-open detection belongs — and the presence of `reopen_count` as a recorded and predictive attribute in real incident data [5] confirms that premature closure is a real phenomenon in operational ITSM rather than a hypothetical risk. Per-step verification and rollback are recorded as future work with an evidence base [2], not claimed as present capability.

**Escalation as a designed outcome rather than a failure.** Every family reviewed retains a human somewhere. Tiered support escalates between tiers; LLM diagnosis systems leave ownership with the on-call engineer [17], [18]; RAG recommenders advise a human agent [11], [12]; VIGIL provides structured escalation to human operators [2]. The field study evidence reinforces why this is appropriate: an assistant that only advised produced a 14% productivity improvement, concentrated among less experienced workers [3]. **Author's assessment:** a system that hands a well-contextualised, partially diagnosed ticket to a human agent has delivered most of the measured value even when it resolves nothing itself. This reframes escalation in the proposed workflow: the objective is not to minimise escalations, but to ensure that an escalated ticket arrives with conversation history, retrieved knowledge, executed actions and their results attached — precisely the context that the conventional tiered workflow discards at each handoff [5]. Evaluating the system on autonomous resolution rate alone would therefore understate what it contributes, and Chapter 1's evaluation design accordingly includes resolution time and user-facing measures alongside it.

**Workflow limitations of existing practice, and the opportunities they create.** Three workflow-level opportunities follow from the comparison above, and each is exploited by a specific element of the proposed design rather than being noted in the abstract.

The first is **continuity of context within a single conversation**. The conventional workflow loses context at every tier transition, and the cost is measurable in the incident record itself, where reassignment count is among the strongest predictors of resolution duration [5]. A workflow in which understanding, retrieval, ticket creation, action and lifecycle tracking all operate over one continuous conversation eliminates the re-elicitation step by construction rather than by improving any individual stage. This is the workflow-level justification for orchestrating the five agents over a shared conversation rather than deploying them as independent services.

The second is **the feedback path from execution back into the record**. In the conventional workflow, what a support engineer tried and what resulted is captured, if at all, as free-text notes written after the fact. In the proposed workflow every approved action, its parameters, its outcome and its timestamp are written to the audit log as they occur. Beyond its accountability function, this produces exactly the class of process-derived attribute that resolution-time prediction depends on — update counts, action counts, reassignment and reopen events [5] — as a by-product of normal operation. **Author's assessment:** this is the strongest workflow argument for integrating prediction with the conversation rather than bolting it on. A standalone predictor must consume whatever the ticketing system happened to record; an integrated one generates its own features at the point of action. The evaluation should therefore examine whether prediction accuracy improves as a session accumulates executed actions, since that is the mechanism by which integration is supposed to pay.

The third is **cost and latency as workflow properties rather than implementation details**. The AIOps survey identifies time-efficiency and cost-effectiveness of LLM-based solutions as a domain-specific challenge, observing that a smaller model combined with human expertise may achieve comparable results at a fraction of the cost, and that inference cost is most critical where a step must run continuously [19]. In the proposed workflow no component runs continuously — every model invocation is triggered by a user message — which places this system in the more favourable regime that [19] identifies. It does not exempt it from the concern: a multi-agent pipeline invokes the model several times per user turn, and the trade-off accepted in Section 2.5.2 for governance reasons is paid in latency at exactly the point where the user is waiting. Response latency is therefore treated in Chapter 1 as a measured quantity rather than an implementation footnote.

## 2.6 Reflection

The purpose of this section is to convert the preceding analysis into a research position: to state what the literature has settled, what it has not, and what this research therefore undertakes.

**What the literature has already solved.** Four things can be treated as established rather than as open questions, and the proposed research builds on them rather than re-investigating them.

First, **AI assistance in support work produces measurable value, and its mechanism is understood**. A staggered deployment across 5,179 agents raised issues resolved per hour by 14%, with a 34% improvement among novice and low-skilled workers and minimal effect on the most experienced, attributable to the dissemination of the working practice of more able agents [3]. The mechanism is improved access to knowledge the organisation already holds — which is also what ITSM process research identifies as a persistent challenge from the process side [1].

Second, **retrieval grounding works, including on real enterprise support corpora**. The mechanism is well characterised [7], [8], its retrieval component is well founded algorithmically [9], [10], and it has been deployed in this exact domain under realistic constraints of knowledge coverage and model size [11], with one production deployment reporting a 28.6% reduction in median per-issue resolution time [12].

Third, **supervised modelling of ticket categorisation and resolution time is feasible and its conditions are known**: 93.12% classification accuracy under appropriate imbalance treatment [4], and R² = 0.8318 for resolution-time prediction when lifecycle features are aggregated [5].

Fourth, **agent decomposition is a well-formed architectural paradigm** with a stable formulation [14], an established reasoning–action control structure [13], and documented application across software engineering [15].

**What remains insufficient.** Five deficiencies persist, and each corresponds to one of the specific problems stated in Chapter 1.

The first is **composition**. Component maturity has not produced system competence: agents built on state-of-the-art models resolve 11.4% of realistic SRE scenarios, 25.2% of compliance scenarios and 25.8% of financial operations scenarios [16], and the failure analysis attributes a substantial share to low-level tool-calling errors rather than to reasoning failures alone [16]. This is the evidence that the deficiency is architectural rather than a matter of model capability.

The second is the **recommendation ceiling**. The strongest domain systems stop at advice and reach performance consistent with assistance rather than autonomy — up to 0.766 root cause accuracy over a year of production incidents [18], with expert validation required to assess quality because lexical metrics cannot [17]. The residual manual work that Chapter 1 identifies as the cost centre is therefore left in place by exactly the systems that are best at diagnosing it.

The third is **measurement**. Retrieval-augmented support systems are predominantly evaluated on retrieval and answer quality rather than on whether the user's problem was resolved [11], [12]; metric suites now exist that decompose retrieval and generation quality properly [25], but decomposition of *quality* is not the same as measurement of *outcome*. The field-level survey documents inconsistent evaluation methodology across 183 papers as a systemic problem [19].

The fourth is **characterisation of integrated systems**. VIGIL demonstrates that the composed, consent-governed workflow is viable and valuable — 39% fewer interaction rounds, at least fourfold faster diagnosis, 82% self-service resolution in matched cases [2]. But its results are reported at system level with no decomposition establishing which components produce them, its case-matching analysis covers 39% of sessions, its response quality was assessed by automated LLM evaluation rather than expert judgement, its survey drew 23 responses from 100 participants, and it ran on a single device category [2]. Its own counter-intuitive finding — higher user ratings when no knowledge-base match existed [2] — demonstrates that component contributions cannot be inferred from aggregate outcomes.

The fifth is **the instantiation of safety controls inside a domain support system**. That tool-using systems are exposed to direct and indirect prompt injection is established beyond reasonable dispute: retrieved content can carry the attack [20], 31 of 36 tested real-world applications were susceptible [21], detector-based defences reduce attack success to 8% rather than to zero [22], and even the safest evaluated agent fails in 23.9% of test cases [23]. These results are published as security findings and design principles. What is not published is an IT support system in which the resulting controls are specified as part of the architecture and tested against adversarial input as part of its evaluation.

**The research gap.** These five deficiencies are not independent observations; they describe one condition. Every component required for governed, end-to-end IT support automation has been demonstrated somewhere, and the two systems that assemble them into an operational whole [2], [12] report outcomes without publishing architectures that can be inspected, reimplemented or evaluated component by component. The gap is therefore not the absence of capability but the absence of a design that is open to examination:

> There exists no *openly documented and independently evaluable* integrated IT support platform that combines LLM-driven conversational intelligence, RAG-based knowledge retrieval, human-gated system remediation, intelligent multi-agent ticket management, visual analysis of error screenshots, and predictive analytics within a single secure and auditable framework.

This wording is the strongest claim the evidence permits. A stronger formulation — that no such integrated platform exists — is refuted by [2]. A weaker one would not identify anything worth researching. Stating it at this level also makes it falsifiable, which is the property that distinguishes a research gap from an assertion.

**What this research addresses, and how the gap motivates it.** Each element of the proposed design in Chapter 1 now traces to a specific deficiency identified above rather than to a technology preference. Multi-agent decomposition responds to the composition deficiency and, more importantly, creates the boundaries at which execution can be constrained independently of conversation [14], [15], [20]–[23]. Retrieval grounding before dialogue responds to the knowledge-access constraint [1], [3] using the mechanism whose value in this domain is evidenced [11], [12], ordered as the strongest production evidence recommends [18]. The whitelist, parameter validation and universal approval gate respond to the safety deficiency with enforcement located where no model output can reach it [20]–[23], accepting a lower automation ceiling than [2] because no fleet-level observability compensates at this scale. Ticket intelligence and predictive estimation respond to the manual-management and reactivity problems using methods of demonstrated feasibility [4], [5], scoped to what the feature-sensitivity evidence supports. And the evaluation design responds to the measurement deficiency by separating retrieval quality from resolution outcome [25], comparing against a matched internal baseline rather than an incommensurable published average, and testing the approval pathway against adversarial input rather than assuming it holds [20], [21], [22].

**The limits of what this research can claim.** Three limitations follow from the same literature and are stated here so that Chapter 5 reports them rather than discovers them. Evidence from a deployed IT support RAG system shows that synthetic support questions written by subject-matter experts are systematically cleaner than genuine user cases, which are more verbose and carry more off-topic noise [11]; results obtained on the synthetic corpus used in this study are therefore optimistic relative to deployment. The proposed workflow verifies after execution rather than after each step and does not implement rollback, which is weaker than the state of the art [2]. And this research does not claim to outperform commercial platforms or the industrial pilot in [2]; its claim is that an integrated, governed architecture for AI-driven IT support can be built, documented and evaluated openly at a scale that permits reimplementation — which is what the reviewed literature does not currently provide.

Chapter 3 sets out the research methodology through which this artefact is designed, developed and evaluated.
