## 3.1 Research Paradigm

A research paradigm is the set of philosophical assumptions that determines what counts as knowledge in a study, what kind of evidence is admissible, and how conclusions may legitimately be drawn from that evidence. The paradigm must therefore be selected to fit the problem, not chosen first and applied to whatever problem follows.

The problem established in Chapter 1 has a specific character. It is not a question about the state of the world that could be answered by observing it more carefully: the relevant facts are already documented. ITSM process research records that maintaining findable resolution knowledge is a persistent difficulty rather than a solved administrative matter [1]; field measurement shows that improving access to that knowledge raises support throughput by 14%, with the gain concentrated among the least experienced agents [3]; and benchmark evidence shows that assembling capable components into a system that reliably completes realistic IT work remains largely unachieved, with agents resolving 11.4% of realistic site reliability engineering scenarios [16]. What is missing is not an observation. What is missing is an artefact — a designed system that composes these capabilities under governance — together with knowledge about whether and under what conditions such an artefact works.

**Selected paradigm: pragmatism, operationalised through Design Science Research.**

Pragmatism holds that the value of a proposition lies in its practical consequences, and that a research design should be chosen for its capacity to answer the question at hand rather than for its allegiance to a philosophical position. This is appropriate here because the research question in Chapter 1 asks *how* a system can be designed, implemented and evaluated, which is a question about construction and consequence rather than about the nature of an observed phenomenon. Pragmatism additionally licenses the mixed evidence this study requires — quantitative measurement of system behaviour alongside qualitative judgement of whether a user's problem was actually resolved — without requiring the two to be reconciled to a single epistemology.

Pragmatism alone, however, is a stance rather than a method. It is operationalised in this research through **Design Science Research (DSR)**, the information systems paradigm in which knowledge is produced by building and evaluating an artefact that addresses a relevant organisational problem. Hevner et al. establish the two conditions that make such work research rather than engineering: the artefact must address a demonstrably relevant problem, and it must be rigorously evaluated, with the resulting knowledge communicated so that others can build on it [26]. Both conditions are directly satisfiable in this study, and both are already committed to in Chapter 1 — problem relevance is evidenced in §1.2 from measured sources, evaluation is objective 1.7.4, and communication at a level permitting reimplementation is the stated contribution of §1.3.2.

**Why alternative paradigms were not selected.** Two alternatives were considered, and each was rejected for a reason specific to this problem rather than on general grounds.

A **positivist** paradigm treats knowledge as arising from objective measurement of an independently existing reality, typically through hypothesis testing under controlled conditions. Elements of this study are positivist in character — classification accuracy, retrieval relevance and resolution time are measured quantitatively, and §3.2 does derive testable expectations from the literature. But a strictly positivist design would require the object of study to exist before the research begins. Here it does not: the artefact must first be constructed, and the construction itself embodies design decisions that constitute part of the contribution [26]. Positivism can govern the evaluation stage of this study; it cannot govern the whole of it.

An **interpretivist** paradigm treats knowledge as socially constructed and seeks to understand meaning from the perspective of participants. This has genuine relevance at the margins of the problem — user trust in an AI system that acts on their machine is a subjective matter, and the pilot evidence reviewed in Chapter 2 found that users identified transparency of the system's actions as critical to trust [2]. But interpretivism would direct the study towards understanding support staff and end users rather than towards building and testing a system, which is not what the research question asks. Its contribution here is properly confined to the qualitative component of the evaluation.

**How the selected paradigm shapes each element of this research.**

| Element of the research | Consequence of adopting pragmatism operationalised through DSR |
|---|---|
| **Perspective on the problem** | The problem is treated as a deficiency to be remedied by construction, not a phenomenon to be described. The unit of inquiry is the Auto-Ops-AI artefact and the design decisions embodied in it, and the standard of success is whether the constructed system addresses the six specific problems stated in §1.3.2 |
| **Type of evidence collected** | Mixed by necessity. Quantitative system behaviour (resolution time, classification accuracy, retrieval relevance, prediction error, latency) establishes what the artefact does; qualitative human judgement establishes whether what it did constituted a resolution — a distinction that matters because the largest published study of LLM incident diagnosis states explicitly that automatic text metrics cannot substitute for expert assessment of actual performance [17] |
| **Analysis performed** | Analysis is comparative and diagnostic rather than purely descriptive. Measurements are interpreted against a matched manual baseline and against published component baselines where one exists [4], and negative or partial results are treated as findings about the design rather than as failures to be excluded |
| **Design and development activity** | Development is part of the research method, not preparation for it. Each design decision recorded in §2.5.2 is traceable to reviewed evidence, so that the artefact functions as an argument that can be examined, and its construction is documented to a level permitting independent reimplementation |
| **Evaluation of the solution** | Evaluation is conducted against the objectives of §1.7 rather than against a general notion of quality, is performed on a defined scenario set with human-authored ground truth, and reports the conditions under which results were obtained — a response to the field-level finding that AIOps and LLM-based IT research suffers from inconsistent evaluation methodology across the literature [19] |

The paradigm is therefore not adopted because DSR is conventional in information systems dissertations. It is adopted because this study produces an artefact whose value is established by its consequences, which is precisely the class of inquiry DSR was formulated to govern [26].

## 3.2 Research Approach

The research approach concerns the logical relationship between theory and evidence: whether the study reasons from observations towards a general explanation, from existing theory towards testable expectations, or from a surprising observation towards the most plausible explanation of it. Three candidate approaches were assessed against the actual sequence of work this research performs.

**Inductive reasoning** moves from specific observations to general theory, and is appropriate where a phenomenon is poorly characterised and the aim is to generate explanation from data. It is **not suitable as the primary approach here**, for a specific reason: the phenomenon this research addresses is already characterised in the literature. The cost of applying existing organisational knowledge is documented from the process side [1] and measured from the productivity side [3]; the failure of composed systems is measured at benchmark level [16]; the exposure of tool-using systems to prompt injection is established empirically [20], [21]. Collecting fresh observations in order to generate a theory that the reviewed literature already supplies would duplicate settled work rather than extend it. Inductive reasoning retains a limited role at the evaluation stage, where unanticipated failure modes observed during testing may generate explanations that were not derived from theory beforehand.

**Abductive reasoning** moves from an observation to the most plausible explanation or solution that would account for it. This is the reasoning that produced the proposed design, and Interim Submission 01 correctly identifies it as the study's initial mode. The observation is the "action gap" identified in Chapter 2: four of the five families of existing system populate the workflow stages up to diagnosis and stop before resolution, verification and closure, and the family that does act reports its results only in aggregate [2]. From this the most plausible design response is inferred — a decomposed multi-agent architecture in which the conversational component and the executing component are separable, so that execution can be constrained independently of conversation [14], [15], [20]–[23]. Abduction is the appropriate logic for this stage because a design proposal is not deduced from theory and is not induced from data; it is inferred as the arrangement that would best account for the deficiency observed.

**Deductive reasoning** moves from existing theory to specific expectations that are then tested empirically. Once the architecture is proposed, this is the appropriate logic, because the reviewed literature supports specific expectations about how the constructed system should behave. These expectations, restated here in a form the evaluation can actually assess, are:

1. Retrieval grounding will improve the organisational appropriateness of generated troubleshooting advice relative to ungrounded generation, since grounding is the established mitigation for hallucination [7], [8] and has been shown to improve outcomes on real enterprise support corpora [11], [12].
2. Enforcing the action whitelist, parameter validation and approval gate in application code will prevent execution of out-of-whitelist operations under adversarial input, since defences expressed inside the model's context reduce but do not eliminate injection success [22], [23].
3. Resolution-time and SLA-risk estimates will be more accurate when computed from features accumulated during a session than from features available at ticket creation, following the lifecycle-aggregation finding of [5].
4. Assisted sessions will complete faster than matched manual sessions on the same scenarios, consistent with the direction — though not the magnitude — of the measured effect of support assistance [3].

**Selected approach: an abductive–deductive sequence within a pragmatist stance.** The two are not alternatives but consecutive stages of one argument: abduction produces the design, deduction tests the expectations that follow from it, and the pragmatist stance determines that both are judged by consequence. This sequence maps directly onto the research objectives of §1.7 — objectives 1.7.1 and 1.7.2 (identify and analyse) supply the observations from which the design is abduced, objective 1.7.3 (design and develop) instantiates it, and objective 1.7.4 (evaluate) tests the deduced expectations.

A purely pragmatic approach without this internal structure was considered and rejected as insufficiently specific. Pragmatism answers the question of what makes a claim valuable; it does not by itself state how the claim is to be reached, and a methodology chapter that stopped at "whatever works" would not tell a reader how the design was arrived at or how it will be judged. The abductive–deductive sequence supplies that specificity while remaining consistent with the pragmatist paradigm selected in §3.1.

## 3.3 Research Strategy

**Selected strategy: applied system development (prototyping) paired with scenario-based experimental evaluation.**

The strategy is the practical plan through which the approach is carried out. This research does not conduct a survey of practitioners, an observational case study of a support organisation, or a purely theoretical analysis, because none of these would produce the artefact that the research question requires. Instead it engineers the Auto-Ops-AI prototype and then subjects it to controlled scenarios in a simulated enterprise environment.

**What the strategy involves.** The development component constructs the system specified in §1.7.3 and §1.8: the React and Vite frontend, the FastAPI backend with JWT authentication and role-based access control, the orchestration layer coordinating the five specialised agents (LLM Conversation, Ticket Intelligence, Action Executor, Image Analysis, Ticket Status), the RAG engine over the ChromaDB vector store, the PowerShell execution engine bounded by the enumerated action whitelist, the SQLite data layer with audit logging, and the predictive models. The evaluation component then executes a defined set of synthetic IT support scenarios — representative of the recurrent categories identified in Chapter 1, such as network connectivity faults, endpoint performance degradation, software configuration errors and access problems — measuring the system's behaviour and comparing it against a matched manual baseline performed on the same scenarios.

**Why this strategy is appropriate to this problem.** Three reasons, each traceable to the analysis in Chapter 2.

First, the research gap concerns an *architecture*, and an architecture cannot be evaluated without being built. The deficiency identified in §2.6 is not that the components are unavailable but that no openly documented composition of them exists whose parts can be assessed individually. Prototyping is the only strategy that produces such an object.

Second, scenario-based evaluation with defined ground truth is the established method for assessing agentic IT systems. ITBench evaluates agents across 102 real-world IT automation scenarios with interpretable metrics precisely because aggregate deployment statistics do not reveal where a system fails [16], and its separation of high-level reasoning errors from low-level tool errors is only possible because the scenarios are controlled and instrumented [16]. The same logic applies at the support tier at a much smaller scale.

Third, a controlled scenario set is what makes the comparison in §1.6 defensible. A field deployment would produce more realistic data but would not permit the same scenario to be performed twice — once with system assistance and once without — which is the arrangement that removes population and task-mix differences as confounds. The strongest field study available for reference achieved this by observing a staggered rollout across thousands of agents [3], a design that is entirely out of reach at undergraduate scale; the scenario-matched alternative is the achievable substitute.

**How the strategy will be applied.** Development proceeds in timeboxed iterations (§3.6), with each agent exercised independently before integration, so that a defect can be localised to a component rather than attributed to the system as a whole. Evaluation is conducted after integration, in an isolated Windows virtual machine with snapshot capability, restored to an identical baseline between trials, because remediation actions mutate system state and uncontrolled state drift would make timing measurements incomparable (§1.9.1). Each scenario is executed under two conditions — assisted and manual — using the same task description, the same scoring criteria and the same timing protocol, with system assistance as the manipulated variable.

**How the strategy supports the objectives.** Objective 1.7.3 is satisfied by the development component; objective 1.7.4 is satisfied by the experimental component; and objectives 1.7.1 and 1.7.2 are satisfied by the literature-based fact collection described in §3.4, which precedes and constrains both. The strategy is therefore not an addition to the objectives but the mechanism by which they are met.

**A stated limitation of the strategy.** Scenario-based evaluation trades realism for control, and the trade is not free. Evidence from a deployed IT support RAG system shows that synthetic support questions authored by subject-matter experts are systematically cleaner than genuine user cases, which are more verbose and contain more off-topic noise [11]. Results obtained under this strategy are therefore expected to be optimistic relative to deployment. This is accepted because the alternative — evaluating on production support transcripts — is not ethically or practically available at this scale (§1.10), and it is disclosed here so that Chapter 5 reports it as a boundary on the findings rather than discovering it afterwards.

## 3.4 Fact Collection Mechanisms

This research draws on four distinct classes of evidence. They are distinguished here because they serve different objectives, carry different reliability risks, and are collected at different stages of the study.

### 3.4.1 Secondary evidence: peer-reviewed research literature

**What is collected.** Published research on ITSM practice, supervised machine learning applied to ticket records, LLM-based incident diagnosis, retrieval-augmented generation and its retrieval components, LLM-based agent architectures, agent benchmarks, and the security of tool-using language-model applications — the corpus critically analysed in Chapter 2.

**Why it is needed.** This evidence discharges objectives 1.7.1 and 1.7.2 in full. It establishes the problem's relevance, identifies the limitations of existing approaches, supplies the requirements the artefact must satisfy, and justifies each design decision recorded in §2.5.2. It also supplies the published component baseline against which classification performance will be compared [4].

**How reliability is handled.** Three controls are applied. Sources are preferred where they report measurement on real data at scale rather than assertion — which is why the evidence base is weighted towards production studies [12], [18], field measurement [3], benchmarks [16] and systematic reviews [1], [19]. Preprints are used where they are the only available form of recent and directly relevant work but are marked as such in the reference list. And each source is read in full rather than cited from an abstract, so that reported limitations — such as the case-matching coverage and self-selection caveats stated by the authors of [2] — are carried into the analysis rather than omitted from it.

### 3.4.2 System and technical information

**What is collected.** Documentation and behavioural characteristics of the technologies specified in §1.9.2: the Google Gemini API and its embedding model, the ChromaDB vector store, FastAPI, SQLAlchemy and SQLite, the scikit-learn modelling libraries, the React and Vite frontend toolchain, and Windows PowerShell as the execution surface for remediation actions.

**Why it is needed.** Objective 1.7.3 requires a working system, and the feasibility of each design decision depends on what the selected technologies actually support. This class of evidence also determines what must be recorded for reproducibility — most importantly the hosted model version, since results conditioned on an unrecorded version are not reproducible if the provider updates the model silently (§1.9.2).

**How reliability is handled.** Technical claims that affect results are verified by execution rather than by documentation: where the system's behaviour is asserted in Chapter 4, the assertion is checked against the running implementation. Version numbers of all components are recorded with the evaluation results.

### 3.4.3 Primary evidence: system performance logs

**What is collected.** Automatically instrumented measurements produced during evaluation trials: ticket resolution time, message classification outcomes, RAG retrieval results and relevance scores, resolution-time and SLA-risk predictions with their realised outcomes, proposed and executed actions with their parameters and results, approval decisions, escalation events, lifecycle transitions, and end-to-end response latency.

**Why it is needed.** These are the quantitative measures named in objective 1.7.4. They are collected automatically rather than by manual observation because the audit log required for accountability (§1.8) already records the same events, so instrumentation adds measurement without adding a second, divergent record.

**How reliability and relevance are handled.** Four provisions apply. Measurements are taken in an isolated virtual machine restored to a uniform baseline between trials, so that state drift does not confound timing. Retrieval relevance is recorded separately from end-to-end resolution outcome rather than being treated as a proxy for it, following the distinction that the reviewed domain systems leave unresolved [11], [12]; the decomposition of retrieval and generation quality into faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance provides the instrument for this separation [25]. Prediction accuracy is recorded at more than one point in a session, because the reviewed evidence shows resolution-time prediction to be substantially stronger on lifecycle-aggregated features than on those available at ticket creation [5], and a single measurement point would conceal that dependency. And automatic text-similarity metrics are not used as evidence of resolution quality, because the largest published study of LLM incident diagnosis states that such metrics support relative model comparison but require expert inspection to assess actual performance [17].

### 3.4.4 Primary evidence: matched manual baseline and human assessment

**What is collected.** Three related items. First, timing and outcome data from manual completion of the same scenarios without system assistance, forming the baseline required by §1.6. Second, human judgement of whether each assisted session actually resolved the scenario, scored against criteria defined before the trials. Third, structured questionnaire responses using Likert scales from the sample of test users participating in the evaluation, covering satisfaction, perceived usability and conversational effectiveness, as specified in Interim Submission 01.

**Why it is needed.** Objective 1.7.4 includes qualitative measures, and neither resolution quality nor usability can be established from system logs. The reviewed literature makes the case for human judgement specifically: expert validation is required to assess whether recommended diagnosis is genuinely useful [17], and the closest integrated system to this proposal assessed its own response quality by automated LLM evaluation rather than human expert judgement and records this as a limitation of its findings [2].

**How reliability and relevance are handled.** Scoring criteria and the scenario set are defined before data collection, so that outcomes are not scored to fit results. The number of participants and the response rate are reported with the results rather than omitted, because the same pilot found self-selection to be a genuine threat when only 23 of 100 participants responded [2]. Participation is voluntary and informed (§3.6.2). The sample available at undergraduate scale is small, so questionnaire responses are treated as indicative of user experience rather than as statistically generalisable, and no inferential claim is made from them that the sample size cannot support.

### 3.4.5 Primary evidence: adversarial testing of the remediation pathway

**What is collected.** The outcome of deliberately adversarial inputs directed at the approval and execution pathway — instructions embedded in user messages, in text within uploaded screenshots, and in knowledge base content retrieved by the RAG engine — recording in each case whether any operation outside the whitelist was executed and whether execution occurred without a logged user approval.

**Why it is needed.** Objective 1.7.4 requires that the safety of the remediation pathway be tested rather than assumed. The requirement follows from the evidence reviewed in §2.5.2: retrieved content can itself carry an attack [20], 31 of 36 tested real-world LLM-integrated applications were found susceptible to injection [21], and detector-based defences reduce attack success to 8% rather than to zero [22].

**How reliability and relevance are handled.** The test is scoped to what a study at this scale can honestly claim. It is not a security audit and does not establish that the system is secure; it establishes whether the specific enforcement property claimed in §1.8 — that no model output can cause an out-of-whitelist operation to execute, and that nothing executes without logged approval — holds under the injection techniques described in the reviewed literature. Methodologically this follows the separation used by AgentDojo, which evaluates task utility and security properties independently rather than reporting a single combined score [22], and the practice of validating surfaced failures by human review, as ToolEmu does when it reports that 68.8% of the failures its emulator surfaces are confirmed as genuine risks [23]. Test cases and outcomes are recorded so that the claim can be checked.

### 3.4.6 Data sources for the knowledge base and evaluation corpus

**What is collected.** A curated synthetic and representative enterprise IT support dataset comprising user profiles, knowledge base articles covering the recurrent issue categories, and historical tickets with associated conversations, used to populate the ChromaDB vector store and to support the predictive models, as specified in §1.10.

**Why it is needed.** The RAG engine is meaningless without an organisational corpus to retrieve from, and the predictive models require historical tickets with realised resolution times.

**How reliability and relevance are handled.** Two provisions follow directly from the reviewed literature. First, the corpus is constructed to include the noise characteristics of genuine user reports as far as is practicable, because the team that deployed a comparable system found synthetic expert-authored questions to be materially cleaner than real cases and moved to real ticket data as a result [11]; where this cannot be fully achieved, the residual optimism is reported as a limitation rather than concealed. Second, class balance across issue categories is recorded and considered when interpreting classification results, because the published comparison of ticket-classification algorithms shows that performance depends materially on imbalance treatment and corpus size rather than being an intrinsic property of the method [4]. No production data from any live enterprise environment is used (§1.10).

## 3.5 Research Methodology Execution Workflow

This section sets out how the research is executed from problem identification through to communication of findings. The sequence follows the Design Science Research process model of Peffers et al., whose six activities — problem identification and motivation, definition of objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication — provide the standard structure for design-oriented information systems research [27]. The six stages below correspond to those activities, with demonstration and evaluation combined in §3.5.6 because in this study the same scenario trials serve both purposes.

| Research activity | How it is addressed in this research | Output / result |
|---|---|---|
| **3.5.1 Problem identification** | Structured review of ITSM process research, field measurement of AI-assisted support work, and benchmark evidence on agentic IT systems, to establish what the actual constraint on support work is | The problem statement of §1.3: rising demand against bounded capacity, with resolution knowledge that exists but is costly to apply |
| **3.5.2 Relevance justification** | The problem's significance is established from measured, peer-reviewed evidence rather than from market or vendor projections, and the deficiency is shown to be architectural rather than a matter of model capability | §1.2 and §1.3.1, with each claim carrying a source that measured it |
| **3.5.3 Comparative analysis and gap justification** | Five families of existing approach are analysed for what they achieve, where they stop, and whether they are suitable for this problem, and their limitations are aggregated into a bounded gap statement | Chapter 2 in full, terminating in the research gap of §2.6 |
| **3.5.4 Define and finalise objectives** | Objectives are derived by tracing problem → gap → research question → aim → objectives, with each objective given an assessable outcome | The four objectives of §1.7, unchanged in this chapter |
| **3.5.5 Design, development and data management** | The five-agent architecture is implemented in timeboxed iterations, each agent exercised independently before integration; the knowledge corpus is constructed and vectorised; the predictive models are trained; the enforcement layer is implemented in application code | The Auto-Ops-AI prototype, the populated ChromaDB knowledge store, the SQLite operational and audit store, and the trained predictive models |
| **3.5.6 Evaluation and communication** | The prototype is demonstrated on the defined scenario set and evaluated against the objectives under assisted and matched manual conditions, with adversarial testing of the remediation pathway and a usability questionnaire | Measured results with stated conditions, reported in Chapter 5 and communicated through the thesis and prototype demonstration |

### 3.5.1 Problem Identification

The problem was identified by examining the gap between what enterprise IT support is required to deliver and what it can deliver with human capacity alone, and then by testing the common explanations for that gap against measured evidence.

The starting observation was practical: support teams spend a disproportionate share of their time on repetitive issues whose solutions already exist. Two lines of evidence were then used to determine whether this observation identified the real constraint. ITSM process research documents that establishing and maintaining findable resolution knowledge is among the persistent, unresolved challenges of ITSM implementation rather than an administrative matter [1]. Field measurement supplies the complementary quantity: introducing a generative assistant across 5,179 support agents raised issues resolved per hour by 14%, with a 34% gain among novice and low-skilled workers and minimal effect on the most experienced [3]. The distributional shape of that result is what identified the constraint, because a gain concentrated where organisational knowledge is least internalised indicates a limitation of knowledge access rather than of expressive capability.

The identification was then tested for the obvious alternative explanation — that the problem is simply a shortage of model capability that will resolve as models improve. Benchmark evidence contradicts this: agents built on state-of-the-art models resolve only 11.4% of realistic site reliability engineering scenarios, 25.2% of compliance scenarios and 25.8% of financial operations scenarios [16], while the same class of model performs substantially better on isolated reasoning tasks. The problem was therefore identified as architectural — a deficiency in how capable components are composed and governed — which is the formulation carried into §1.3.

### 3.5.2 Relevance Justification

Relevance in Design Science Research means that the artefact addresses a problem of genuine importance in its application environment, and that the importance is demonstrated rather than asserted [26]. Two decisions govern how relevance is justified in this study.

First, the problem's significance is established from **measurement rather than projection**. Interim Submission 01 justified relevance using market-size figures, industry-survey percentages and analyst forecasts. Those figures were removed in the revised Chapter 1 because their methodology is not published and they cannot be independently verified, and the underlying arguments were re-based on peer-reviewed evidence: the persistence of the knowledge-maintenance problem [1], the measured productivity effect of improved knowledge access [3], the measured shortfall of composed agentic systems [16], and the reported cost of round-trip interaction in a real support deployment, where situating diagnosis at the endpoint reduced interaction rounds by 39% [2]. This substitution does not weaken the relevance argument; it makes it defensible under scrutiny, which is the standard DSR requires.

Second, relevance is established at the **correct tier**. Chapter 2 distinguishes the end-user support tier addressed by this research from the infrastructure operations tier that most published agentic work targets, and notes that a field-level survey of 183 papers documents fragmented architectures and inconsistent evaluation across that adjacent field [19]. Claiming relevance for this study on the strength of infrastructure-tier results would overstate it. The relevance claimed is specific: at the tier where a person reports a problem in natural language, the cost of applying existing knowledge is the binding constraint, and no openly documented architecture composes the capabilities that would reduce it.

### 3.5.3 Comparative Analysis and Gap Justification

The gap was derived, not assumed. The comparative analysis in Chapter 2 examined five families of existing approach against the stages of a complete support workflow, and the gap follows from where those families stop.

Traditional tiered ticketing provides accountability but records work rather than reducing it, with reassignment count itself acting as a measured determinant of resolution duration [5]. Supervised machine learning applied to the ticket record achieves strong measured performance — 93.12% classification accuracy under appropriate imbalance treatment [4], and R² = 0.8318 for lifecycle-aggregated resolution-time prediction against R² = 0.5412 without aggregation [5] — but produces terminal artefacts that do not diagnose, consult knowledge or act. LLM-based incident diagnosis reaches useful but sub-autonomous performance, up to 0.766 root cause accuracy over a year of production incidents [18], and requires expert validation to assess quality [17]. Retrieval-augmented support recommenders demonstrate that grounding works on real enterprise corpora and can shorten resolution time in production [11], [12], but terminate at a recommendation. Integrated agentic systems populate the remaining stages and demonstrate that consent-governed remediation is viable, but report outcomes at system level without decomposing them, on a case-matched subset covering 39% of sessions, assessed by automated rather than expert judgement, on a single device category [2].

Two conclusions were drawn from reading these families against one another, and both bear directly on the methodology. The first is that the deficiency is concentrated at the joints between workflow segments rather than within any segment, which is why the artefact this research builds is an *architecture* rather than an improved component. The second is that the original gap formulation could not be sustained: Interim Submission 01 claimed that no integrated platform of this kind exists, and a single citation refutes that [2]. The gap was accordingly narrowed to the absence of an **openly documented and independently evaluable** integrated platform — a claim the evidence supports, and one that is falsifiable, which is the property that distinguishes a research gap from an assertion.

### 3.5.4 Define and Finalize Objectives

The objectives were derived by following a single chain, in which each element constrains the next.

**Problem** (§1.3.1: demand outgrowing capacity while resolution knowledge remains costly to apply, with six specific shortcomings in §1.3.2) → **Gap** (§1.3.2 and §2.6: no openly documented, independently evaluable integrated platform) → **Research question** (§1.4: how such a system can be designed, implemented and evaluated while maintaining security, auditability and human-in-the-loop control) → **Aim** (§1.6: to design, develop and evaluate Auto-Ops-AI and determine its effect relative to a matched manual baseline) → **Objectives** (§1.7).

The four objectives finalised in Chapter 1 and carried unchanged into this chapter are: **(1) to identify** the challenges and limitations of existing IT support systems and thereby establish the artefact's functional and safety requirements; **(2) to analyse** the capabilities, architectural patterns and documented failure modes of LLMs, RAG and multi-agent systems in the ITSM context; **(3) to design and develop** the five-agent Auto-Ops-AI system with RAG-based retrieval, RBAC, audit logging and a React frontend; and **(4) to evaluate** the developed system quantitatively and qualitatively against a matched manual baseline on the same scenarios.

Two properties of this derivation are methodologically significant. Each objective carries an **assessable outcome** stated in §1.7, so that completion can be demonstrated rather than claimed — a requirement that follows from the DSR obligation to evaluate rigorously [26]. And the fourth objective was **restated during revision** to compare against a matched internal baseline rather than published industry averages for L1 resolution time, because populations, ticket mixes and measurement definitions differ between such figures and this evaluation, and a favourable comparison against an incommensurable figure would be evidence of nothing. The intent of the original objective is preserved; only the comparison that could support it has changed.

### 3.5.5 Design / Development / Data Management and Handling

**Design.** The architecture is the one specified in §1.8 and justified decision by decision in §2.5.2. The layered arrangement places authentication and role-based access control at the API boundary so that authorisation precedes any agent invocation; the orchestration layer routes each request to the agents it requires and writes the audit record itself, so that a record exists independently of whether an agent behaved as designed; the three supporting engines (RAG, dataset analyzer, PowerShell execution) are separated from the agents that invoke them so each can be constrained and tested independently; and the predictive models sit in their own layer because their outputs are advisory signals rather than control decisions.

Three design decisions govern development and are restated here because they determine what is built and how it is tested. The **agents are heterogeneous by design** — generative where unconstrained user language must be interpreted, deterministic where an output feeds an accountable record — which is why the Ticket Status Agent is a rule-based state machine rather than a generative component [6]. The **Action Executor Agent adopts the reason–act–observe control structure** established in the agent literature [13] but replaces free-form command synthesis with selection from an enumerated whitelist plus validated parameters, because benchmark failure analysis shows that unbounded tool interfaces produce invalid calls, syntax errors and repeated invocations [16]. And the **enforcement boundary sits in application code**, not in a prompt, so that no model output can extend the set of executable operations [20], [21], [22], [23].

**Development stages.** Development proceeds in timeboxed iterations (§3.6.1) in the following order: the data layer and authentication with RBAC; the RAG pipeline, comprising knowledge base construction, chunking, embedding via the Gemini embedding model, and ingestion into ChromaDB; the five agents individually; the orchestration layer that composes them; the PowerShell execution engine with whitelist, parameter validation and the approval workflow; the predictive models; and the React frontend surfaces. Each agent is exercised independently against representative inputs before integration, so that a defect observed after integration can be localised to a component rather than attributed to the system as a whole — a practical requirement created by the multi-agent design itself, since a decomposed system has more interfaces at which failure can occur [15].

**Data management and handling.** Four data stores are used, each with a defined role. The **knowledge base** holds curated IT resolution articles in JSON and is the source corpus for retrieval. The **ChromaDB vector store** holds the embedded representations of that corpus and serves semantic search. The **SQLite relational store** holds users, roles, tickets, conversations and lifecycle state. The **audit log** records every proposed action, its parameters, the approval decision, the execution outcome and its timestamp.

Handling follows three principles. **Separation of the corpus from the record**: knowledge base content is read-only at runtime and is versioned, so that a change in retrieval behaviour between evaluation runs can be attributed either to the corpus or to the code, but not ambiguously to both. **Audit completeness before convenience**: the audit record is written by the orchestration layer at the point of decision rather than reconstructed afterwards from conversation text, because a record assembled after the fact cannot serve the accountability function it exists for. And **no production data**: the corpus and ticket history are synthetic and representative, containing no data from any live enterprise environment (§1.10), with the acknowledged consequence — that synthetic support queries are cleaner than genuine ones [11] — reported as a limitation rather than treated as neutral.

The **predictive models** are trained on the synthetic historical ticket data using scikit-learn, with features drawn from both ticket attributes and lifecycle process attributes, following the finding that process-intensity features such as update and reassignment counts are substantially more predictive than attributes available at ticket creation [5]. Class balance across issue categories is recorded and taken into account when interpreting classification results, since published comparison of ticket-classification algorithms shows performance to depend materially on imbalance treatment and corpus size [4].

**Integration and validation activities.** Integration is validated in three stages: component-level exercise of each agent; pipeline-level validation that a message traverses image normalisation, classification, retrieval, dialogue, ticket creation, action proposal, approval and lifecycle transition in the intended order (§1.8); and enforcement-level validation that the whitelist and approval gate hold, including under the adversarial inputs described in §3.4.5. The third stage is treated as a validation activity rather than a security audit, and its claim is bounded accordingly.

### 3.5.6 Evaluation and Communication

**What is evaluated.** Evaluation is conducted against the four objectives rather than against a general notion of system quality, and covers five things: the resolution outcome and time for each scenario; the accuracy of message and ticket classification; the relevance of retrieved knowledge; the accuracy of resolution-time and SLA-risk estimates; and the behaviour of the remediation pathway under both normal and adversarial input. Response latency is measured alongside these, because the multi-agent decomposition adopted for governance reasons invokes the model several times per user turn, and the cost of that decision is paid where the user is waiting [19].

**How it is evaluated.** Each scenario in the defined set is executed under two conditions — with system assistance and manually without it — using the same task description, the same scoring criteria and the same timing protocol, in an isolated virtual machine restored to an identical baseline between trials. Classification performance is compared against the published supervised baseline for IT incident tickets [4], with the caveat that the corpora differ and the comparison is therefore indicative rather than equivalent. Retrieval quality is assessed separately from end-to-end resolution outcome, decomposed into faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance rather than reported as a single score [25]. Prediction accuracy is reported using R² and mean absolute error at more than one point in the session lifecycle, so that the dependence on lifecycle features is visible rather than concealed [5]. Safety is assessed by the injection tests of §3.4.5, reporting utility and security separately in the manner used by AgentDojo rather than as one combined figure [22].

**What evidence is collected.** Instrumented system logs; human scoring of resolution outcome against pre-defined criteria; questionnaire responses from the sample of test users; and the recorded outcomes of the adversarial test cases. All results are recorded together with the conditions under which they were obtained, including the hosted model version, the corpus version and the component versions.

**How results are interpreted.** Three interpretive rules are fixed in advance, each responding to a weakness identified in the reviewed literature. Automatic text-similarity metrics are not treated as evidence that a problem was resolved, because the largest published study of LLM incident diagnosis states that such metrics require expert inspection to assess actual performance [17]. Retrieval relevance is not treated as a proxy for resolution outcome, because the reviewed domain systems leave that relationship unexamined [11], [12] and the one integrated pilot that reported both found users rating the system higher when no knowledge-base match was available — a result that could not have been predicted from retrieval quality alone [2]. And questionnaire responses are reported with the participant count and response rate rather than as a percentage alone, because self-selection is a documented threat in exactly this kind of small-sample evaluation [2].

Results that do not support the deduced expectations of §3.2 are reported as findings about the design rather than excluded. This is a requirement of the paradigm rather than a courtesy: in Design Science Research the evaluation exists to establish what the artefact does, and an evaluation that can only confirm would not discharge the rigour obligation [26].

**What the evaluation can and cannot establish.** It can establish whether the constructed system performs the composed workflow, how its performance compares with matched manual completion of the same scenarios, and whether the enforcement property claimed for the approval gate holds under the tested inputs. It cannot establish deployment performance on genuine user language [11], statistical generalisability from a small participant sample, or superiority over commercial platforms or the industrial pilot reviewed in Chapter 2 [2] — and no such claim is made.

**Communication.** Findings, the architecture, the design rationale and the evaluation results are communicated through this thesis and through the final prototype demonstration. Communication in DSR is not a reporting formality but part of the contribution, since the knowledge produced by a design study resides in the documented artefact and the reasoning behind it [26], [27]. Because the gap identified in §2.6 is specifically the absence of an *openly documented and independently evaluable* architecture, the level of documentation is itself a research output: the component structure, the enforcement mechanism, the scenario set, the scoring criteria and the measurement conditions are reported at a level intended to permit inspection and reimplementation.

## 3.6 Project Management Methodology

**Selected methodology: iterative, timeboxed sprint-based development adapted from Agile Scrum, executed by a single researcher with supervisory review at iteration boundaries.**

The choice is determined by three properties of this specific project rather than by the prevalence of agile methods.

**The requirements are stable but the implementation is uncertain.** What the system must do is fixed by the objectives in §1.7 and the scope in §1.10, and does not change during development. How each part will be built, however, carries real uncertainty: prompt formulations for classification and ticket generation must be arrived at empirically, retrieval chunking and threshold settings must be tuned against the corpus, and the behaviour of a hosted model may change between the start and end of the project — which is why the model version is recorded with every result (§1.9.2). This combination of stable requirements and uncertain implementation is what makes an iterative method appropriate: the value of iteration here is the ability to revise an implementation approach after observing it, not the ability to renegotiate what is being built.

**The system is naturally decomposable into independently testable increments.** The five agents, the RAG pipeline, the enforcement layer, the predictive models and the frontend can each be built and exercised on their own before integration, and §3.5.5 requires exactly this sequencing so that defects can be localised. A method organised around delivering a working increment per iteration therefore matches the technical structure of the work rather than being imposed on it.

**The timeline is fixed and milestone-driven.** The project runs to fixed academic deadlines. A method that timeboxes work and forces a decision about scope at the end of each iteration is better suited to a fixed deadline than one that allows an item to remain in progress indefinitely.

**Why alternatives are less suitable.** A **Waterfall** or **PRINCE2** approach fixes design before implementation and treats change as exception. This does not fit a project in which prompt design, retrieval configuration and model behaviour can only be settled empirically; committing to a complete design before any component has been exercised would produce a specification that development would immediately contradict. **Kanban** is a closer fit, and its emphasis on limiting work in progress is genuinely relevant to a single researcher who can only work on one component at a time. It was not selected as the primary method because Kanban is optimised for a continuous flow of arriving work, whereas this project has a fixed scope and hard milestones, and provides no natural checkpoint at which progress is reviewed against the research objectives. The work-in-progress limit is nonetheless adopted as a working practice within each sprint.

**An honest qualification about Scrum in a single-researcher project.** Canonical Scrum presupposes a development team with distinct Product Owner, Scrum Master and developer roles, daily coordination meetings and collective estimation, none of which is meaningful when one person performs all the work. Claiming to run Scrum would therefore misdescribe what actually happens. What is adopted is the subset of Scrum that remains valid without a team: **timeboxed sprints** with a defined goal, **a working increment at the end of each sprint**, **a review of that increment against the research objectives** — with the project supervisor serving the review function that a stakeholder would otherwise serve — and **a short retrospective** that records what to change in the next sprint. The remaining ceremonies are omitted deliberately rather than by oversight.

### 3.6.1 Project Timeline

The research is structured across four phases over eighteen weeks, aligned with the execution workflow in §3.5.

| Phase | Weeks | Activities | Corresponding workflow stage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 1 — Initiation and foundation** | 1–4 | Problem identification from measured evidence; literature review and critical analysis; gap identification; finalisation of research question, aim and objectives; system architecture design | §3.5.1–§3.5.4; Chapters 1 and 2 |
| **Phase 2 — Core system development** | 5–10 | *Sprint 1:* data layer, SQLite schema, authentication with JWT and RBAC, frontend dashboard scaffold. *Sprint 2:* knowledge base construction, chunking and embedding, ChromaDB ingestion, RAG retrieval pipeline. *Sprint 3:* the five specialised agents, individually exercised, then the orchestration layer; PowerShell execution engine with whitelist, parameter validation and approval workflow. *Sprint 4:* predictive models for resolution time and SLA risk; analytics and admin surfaces | §3.5.5 |
| **Phase 3 — Testing and evaluation** | 11–14 | Deployment to the isolated evaluation environment; execution of the defined scenario set under assisted and matched manual conditions; adversarial testing of the remediation pathway; collection of system logs, human resolution scoring and questionnaire responses; analysis of quantitative and qualitative data | §3.5.6 |

Two dependencies constrain this schedule and are stated so that slippage can be anticipated rather than discovered. Evaluation cannot begin until integration is complete, so any overrun in Sprint 3 compresses Phase 3 directly. And the matched manual baseline requires the same scenario set as the assisted trials, so the scenario set and its scoring criteria must be finalised before Phase 3 begins rather than during it — a sequencing requirement that follows from §3.4.4, where defining criteria in advance is what prevents outcomes from being scored to fit results.

### 3.6.2 Ethical Considerations

Six considerations are genuinely raised by this research. Each arises from a specific feature of the system or the study rather than from generic research-ethics practice.

**1. System safety and non-maleficence.** The system executes commands on a user's machine, so the possibility of causing damage is real rather than theoretical. Three controls address it, all specified in Chapter 1 and justified in Chapter 2. The Action Executor Agent is limited to an enumerated whitelist of non-destructive diagnostic and remediation operations; destructive operations are excluded by scope (§1.10) and are not implementable through the interface; and enforcement occurs in application code rather than by instructing the model, because defences expressed in the model's context can be overridden by injected instructions in the same context [20], [22]. Development and evaluation are conducted in an isolated virtual machine with snapshot capability, so that a defect during development cannot affect a working system.

**2. User consent and transparency.** No remediation action is executed without explicit, logged approval from the user, and the approval request states which action will run and why. This is an ethical requirement and simultaneously a design requirement: the reviewed pilot found that users identified the transparency of the system's actions and reasoning as critical to trust [2], and the agent literature criticises systems that replace human roles rather than supporting human–agent collaboration on precisely the grounds that doing so reduces stakeholder involvement and trust [15]. Users are informed that they are interacting with an AI system.

**3. Responsible use of third-party AI services.** The system sends user messages and uploaded screenshots to a hosted large language model API (§1.9.2). This is a genuine data-handling consideration rather than a formality, because content leaves the local environment. Two provisions apply: all evaluation content is synthetic, so no real personal or organisational data is transmitted; and participants in usability testing are informed that their conversational input is processed by a third-party model service before they take part. This constraint is one of the reasons the reviewed enterprise RAG deployment was designed around smaller models under client cost and privacy restrictions [11], and it is recorded here as a real deployment consideration for any future use of this architecture on production data.

**4. Participant treatment and data privacy.** Participation in the usability evaluation is voluntary and informed, participants may withdraw, and responses are recorded without personally identifying information. All ticket data and user profiles used in the study are synthetic (§1.10); no production support transcripts are used. Within the system, role-based access control restricts visibility of audit logs and ticket data to appropriate roles, so that the accountability record does not itself become a privacy exposure.

**5. Research integrity and transparency of evaluation.** Scenario definitions and scoring criteria are fixed before data collection; results that contradict the expectations in §3.2 are reported rather than excluded; participant counts and response rates are reported alongside questionnaire results; and the conditions under which measurements were taken — including the hosted model version — are recorded with the results. This chapter also records where the revised Chapters 1 and 2 corrected earlier claims: unverifiable industry statistics were removed, and the research gap was narrowed from an absolute claim to a defensible one after a single publication was found to refute the stronger version [2]. Reporting these corrections is part of research integrity rather than an admission against interest, and it responds to a documented field-level problem of inconsistent evaluation practice [19].

**6. Intellectual property and attribution.** The system is built on third-party open-source libraries and a commercial model API, each used within its licence terms, and the knowledge base is constructed for this study rather than reproduced from any organisation's internal documentation. All literature is cited, and no source is represented as supporting a claim it does not make.

## 3.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter has established how the research will actually be conducted, with each methodological decision traced to the problem, gap and objectives fixed in Chapters 1 and 2.

The **research paradigm** is pragmatism, operationalised through Design Science Research, selected because the research question asks how an artefact can be built and evaluated rather than how an existing phenomenon should be understood; positivism and interpretivism were assessed and confined to the roles they can properly serve within the study — quantitative measurement and qualitative user assessment respectively — rather than adopted as the governing paradigm [26]. The **research approach** is an abductive–deductive sequence: abduction produced the multi-agent design as the most plausible response to the workflow deficiency identified in Chapter 2, and deduction supplies four evaluable expectations derived from the reviewed literature. The **research strategy** is applied system development paired with scenario-based experimental evaluation, chosen because an architecture cannot be assessed without being built and because scenario matching is what makes the assisted-versus-manual comparison defensible at this scale.

**Fact collection** draws on four classes of evidence: peer-reviewed literature establishing requirements and design justification; system and technical information determining feasibility and reproducibility; primary quantitative data from instrumented system logs; and primary qualitative data from human resolution scoring, a usability questionnaire and adversarial testing of the remediation pathway. For each, the reliability provisions were stated rather than assumed — in particular the separation of retrieval quality from resolution outcome [25], the refusal to treat automatic text metrics as evidence of resolution quality [17], and the reporting of participant counts and response rates [2].

The **execution workflow** follows the six activities of the Design Science Research process model [27], from problem identification through relevance justification, comparative analysis and gap derivation, objective finalisation, design and development with defined data-handling rules, and finally demonstration, evaluation and communication. **Project management** uses timeboxed sprints adapted from Scrum, with the qualification stated openly that a single-researcher project cannot run canonical Scrum and adopts only the elements that remain meaningful without a team; Waterfall and PRINCE2 were rejected as incompatible with empirically-settled implementation decisions, and Kanban was rejected as primary method because the project has fixed scope and hard milestones rather than continuous arriving work. The **evaluation direction** is comparison against a matched manual baseline on the same scenarios, with quality assessed by human judgement, retrieval assessed separately from outcome, prediction assessed at more than one lifecycle point, and safety assessed by adversarial testing rather than assumption. **Ethical considerations** address system safety, consent and transparency, the transmission of content to a third-party model service, participant treatment and data privacy, research integrity, and intellectual property — each arising from a specific feature of this system rather than from generic practice.

With the methodology established, the following chapter presents the design and implementation of the Auto-Ops-AI system: how the architecture specified in §1.8 and justified in §2.5 is realised in the five specialised agents, the retrieval pipeline, the enforcement layer and the supporting data stores.
